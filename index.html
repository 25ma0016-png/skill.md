Abstract

This project builds an automated writing scoring and evaluation system and reads it through the lens of social theory in the digital era. The tool accepts an essay, marks it out of 100 across five categories (grammar and spelling, vocabulary, sentence structure, content and relevance, punctuation) and returns remarks, strengths, suggestions and highlighted errors. All scoring rules are written openly as simple, countable measures. The assignment uses the working system to ask a wider question: when writing quality is turned into a number, which ideas of “good writing” are built into that number, and who benefits or is disadvantaged by them? The score is an automated estimate, not a human judgement.

1. Objective

  • To design a free, transparent and beginner-friendly system that scores written essays automatically.
  • To show how language measures such as word counts, sentence lengths and error rates can be combined into one score.
  • To examine, using ideas from digital humanities and social theory, what such automated evaluation includes, rewards and leaves out.

2. Research question

Main question: How does an automated writing evaluator translate “good writing” into numbers, and what social and cultural assumptions are encoded in that translation?

Supporting questions:

  • Which features of a text can be measured reliably by simple rules, and which cannot?
  • How does making the marking scheme visible change the way users trust or question a machine-generated score?
  • What risks arise if such scores are used alone, without a teacher or reviewer?

3. Methodology

The study follows a design-and-critique approach: a working prototype is built first, and then analysed as a cultural and social object.

  • Input: text is pasted or loaded from a .txt file. Texts must be between 30 and 5,000 words.
  • Text processing: the essay is split into paragraphs, sentences and words using regular expressions, a basic form of natural language processing (Bird et al., 2009).
  • Measurement: the system counts rule-based errors per 100 words, measures vocabulary variety (a moving-average type-token ratio), the share of long words, sentence-length statistics, linking words and topic keywords.
  • Scoring: each measure is mapped to marks with simple linear scales and if rules, and the marks are added to give a total out of 100.
  • Feedback: remarks (Excellent, Very Good, Good, Average, Needs Improvement), strengths, suggestions and highlighted issues are generated from the same measures.
  • Critical reading: the marking rules are then analysed against literature on automated essay scoring and on bias in algorithmic systems.

The browser version uses built-in grammar rules. The Python version of the project uses LanguageTool (Naber, 2003) for fuller grammar checking.

        5. Analysis

        The marking rules show clearly what the system treats as quality.

        • Quality becomes quantity. Every category reduces to a count or ratio. Errors per 100 words, average sentence length and vocabulary variety are easy to compute, so they become the definition of quality.
        • Proxies stand in for meaning. Linking words such as “however” and “for example” earn marks, but using them does not prove the argument is sound. Length earns marks up to 300 words, which rewards longer writing as such. Perelman (2014) makes a similar criticism of scoring systems that end up counting words.
        • A preferred style is built in. Sentences of 12 to 22 words, longer words and varied openings are rewarded. A concise, plain or deliberately short style can lose marks even when it is effective.
        • A language norm is built in. The grammar rules and spelling list assume one standard form of English. Users must choose British or American spelling in the full version, and regional or dialect forms may be flagged as errors.
        • Relevance is keyword matching. Topic relevance checks whether topic words appear, not whether the essay answers the question.
        • Transparency is a strength. Because each rule is visible, a user can see why a mark was lost and can challenge the rule, which is harder with opaque machine-learning scorers.

        6. Discussion

        Automated essay scoring has a long history, from early proposals to grade essays by computer (Page, 1966) to current large-scale systems (Shermis & Burstein, 2013). Its main attraction is speed and consistency, and its main risk is that what can be measured quietly replaces what matters. Williamson et al. (2012) argue that automated scores need a framework for evaluation and appropriate use, not just technical accuracy.

        From a digital humanities view, the tool is an example of the computational turn (Berry, 2011): humanistic activities such as reading and judging writing are being re-expressed as computation. This brings benefits, such as instant feedback for learners who do not have a tutor, but it also changes what counts as skill. Writers may start to write for the scorer, for example by adding linking words to gain marks.

        From a social theory view, algorithmic systems are not neutral. Noble (2018) and Benjamin (2019) show how automated systems can reproduce existing social inequalities under a surface of objectivity. For writing evaluation, this means a single standard of correctness can disadvantage writers whose language, region or schooling differs from the norm in the rules. Keeping the rules open and the output labelled as an estimate is a practical response: the system supports a teacher’s judgement and does not replace it.

        7. Limitation

        • It cannot understand meaning, so a well-formed essay with wrong facts can still score well.
        • Creativity, humour, voice, tone and the strength of an argument are not measured.
        • The browser version uses built-in grammar rules that catch common errors only, and it may flag correct sentences or miss real mistakes.
        • Relevance depends on keywords, not on real understanding of the topic.
        • The thresholds (for example 12 to 22 words per sentence) are design choices and have not been validated against human markers.
        • The browser version reads .txt files and pasted text only, and it handles English only.

        8. Future work

        • Compare the system’s scores with marks given by teachers on the same essays, to test how well the scheme agrees with human judgement.
        • Add support for .docx files and for downloadable Word reports in the browser version.
        • Test the system on writing in Indian English and in other regional varieties, and adjust the rules to reduce language-norm bias.
        • Improve relevance checking using semantic similarity instead of keyword matching.
        • Study how students change their writing after seeing automated scores, as a question for classroom research.
